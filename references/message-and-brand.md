# Company message and authentic identity

The experience is an interactive expression of the company's message.
Brand assets answer "whose experience is this?" The narrative and actions
answer "what should I understand about this company?" Neither replaces
the other.

## Research before asking for a message

Use official sources to distinguish the company's stated promise from
verifiable evidence about its products/services. A company's aspiration is
not proof that it achieves a result. Do not invent superiority, exclusive
capabilities, customer outcomes, medical effects or environmental savings.
Choose evidence relevant to this audience and brief, not an arbitrary
history/statistics list.

If the person only names a company, propose a credible message and explain
why its real offerings support it. Do not demand that they supply a
strategy document. Preserve a specified hero or scene; find the message
it can carry rather than replacing it with a more convenient subject.

## Compact brief to confirm

Keep this in the conversation or an existing artifact; do not create
planning documents by default. Populate what research and context answer,
separating the person's requirements from recommendations:

- **Company, audience and scope:** intended visitor when known; named
  products/services, exact variants/region and fixed scene/gesture.
- **One message:** "After exploring, the visitor should understand that
  [company] offers [benefit/approach], demonstrated by [actual offering]."
- **Two or three proof points:** actual products/services, supported facts
  and their official URLs. Describe claims with appropriate qualification.
- **Actual identity:** authentic logo, product/service reference images,
  identifying features, asset files, provenance and permitted use.
- **Visitor journey:** opening promise, the actions that demonstrate it,
  and the final company takeaway.
- **Gaps:** only consequential uncertainty requiring a decision. Confirm
  message and asset approach together when possible instead of repeating
  settled questions.

A simulation objective ("operate this device"), catalogue objective
("visit every item"), or slogan alone is not a company message. A requested
training task remains valid, but the brief should make that intent explicit.
For an openly fictional/non-company brief, confirm the intended takeaway
instead of forcing a corporate message or real-company assets.

## Identity and rights are separate decisions

Resolve missing assets, variants and reuse permission before making polished
mockups dependent on them. Official publication establishes provenance,
not a blanket licence. Look for applicable press/brand-kit permissions,
ask for authorised files, or ask whether the person has permission for
the specific use. Record its scope; do not infer permission from internal
use, a disclaimer, public availability or a wish to use a real brand.

Use an authentic permitted logo rather than typed lettering. Preserve real
product identities and characteristic forms/packaging even in stylised
geometry; label a reconstruction illustrative, not an official model.
Authorised user-supplied assets need recorded provenance and identity checks,
not blind relabelling of unknown artwork as official.

Do not recommend an unbranded substitute as the normal way to finish a
branded customer task. If rights remain unresolved, report the brand gate
blocked. A deliberately agreed unbranded wireframe or interaction prototype
can answer a design question, but cannot pass brand authenticity or be
presented as the finished branded experience. Approval of an illustrative
style does not by itself approve removing identity.

## Every major action carries meaning

For each core moment record:

`visitor action -> observable consequence -> company meaning/benefit -> official proof`

The consequence should help explain the meaning. Playing a sourced but
unrelated fact after a gesture is still decorative information. Ask what
the visitor could infer from the action without reading the author brief.
Keep necessary explanatory text near the relevant response, readable and
revisitable; do not rely on the Sources panel to tell the company story.

Frame the company's promise at entry, demonstrate it through a few coherent
moments, and connect them in the ending. A generic completion message does
not close that story. Keep the tone appropriate to the approved brief:
clear communication does not require advertising hype or an unrequested
sales call to action.

Large ranges may be explored in depth without making every entry a
mandatory stage. Retain the complete approved catalogue, product IDs and
discoverability; distinguish main-story evidence from optional exploration.
Complexity should support the takeaway. Do not add orientation puzzles,
precise timing or simulated procedures merely to make an interaction
"realistic" unless that learning requirement is explicit.

## Review the delivered meaning

Read/play the rendered opening, meaningful intermediate states and ending.
Check the approved message against what is actually visible and experienced,
not a declaration in an implementation report.

When a reviewer is available, show the experience before revealing its
message brief. Ask what company it represents, what it wants visitors to
understand, and which actions/evidence conveyed that. Distinguish a
reviewer/agent interpretation from observed human audience comprehension;
neither an assertion count nor the author's own summary proves the latter.

If removing the logo leaves a generic activity, check whether actual
offerings and evidence make the story company-specific. Do not invent
market uniqueness just to pass this test. If the visitor remembers only
the gesture or item count, the message needs revision.
